ELECTRICAL, LIGHTING, FAN, OUTLET, SWITCH, AND ACCESSORY GUIDANCE

This file teaches Junior Andrew how to answer questions about electrical work, ceiling fans, lights, outlets, switches, fan beams, raceways, bays, rows, and accessories.

Main rule:
Do not automatically say an electrician must handle the electrical side.
It is okay to say an electrician can help, but do not imply it is always required.

Better wording:
"You can have an electrician do that if you want, but many customers handle that part themselves. Just make sure it follows local wiring codes, product instructions, and any permit requirements."

Electrical work can depend on:
- local electrical code
- permit requirements
- product instructions
- whether the patio cover includes a fan beam
- whether the customer is adding lights, fans, outlets, switches, heaters, or other accessories
- whether wiring needs to be hidden inside an insulated panel or run externally
- whether the customer is comfortable doing electrical work
- whether the building department requires licensed work

Do not give step-by-step wiring instructions.
Do not tell customers exactly how to wire a fan, outlet, light, switch, or circuit.
Do not give breaker sizing, wire gauge, grounding, junction box, or code-specific electrical instructions as final.
Do not tell someone to do electrical work if they are not comfortable with it.
Do not tell someone to ignore permits or local code.

ELECTRICAL IS POSSIBLE ON ALL COVER TYPES

Fans, lights, outlets, and switches are possible on the different patio cover types and Haven Lanais, but the best way to plan them depends on the product and design.

This includes:
- non-insulated patio covers
- insulated patio covers
- lattice covers, depending on the accessory and layout
- combination covers
- Haven Lanais

Good answer:
"Fans, lights, outlets, and switches are all possible, but they should be planned into the design so the right support, wiring path, and accessories are included."

PKD sells lights, but does not sell the fans.

Safe wording:
"PKD sells the lights, but not the fans. If you want the current light specs, it is best to check with a representative because the exact light we carry can change."

Do not say:
"We sell fans."
"We sell multiple light options" unless confirmed.
Use singular wording like "the current light" or "the light PKD currently carries" because PKD usually sells one light type at a time.

BAY AND ROW TERMINOLOGY

For Patio Kits Direct wording, "bay" and "row" can mean the same thing in this lighting and fan context.

A bay/row is the line of electrical accessories arranged front-to-back along the projection.

Use customer-friendly wording:
"bay"
"row"
"light bay"
"fan bay"

A light bay can commonly be:
- 1 light bay
- 2 light bay
- 3 light bay

A fan bay usually means the fan is the main item in that bay.

If the projection is not deep enough for a 3-light bay, then a fan by itself is often enough for that bay.

If the projection is deep enough where a 3-light bay would make sense, then a common fan bay layout can be:
- one light in front
- one fan centered
- one light behind

This is basically the same front-to-back spacing logic as a 3-light bay, but the center light is replaced by a fan.

Good wording:
"If the projection is deep enough for a 3-light bay, a common fan bay is one light in front, the fan centered, and one light behind it. If the projection is not deep enough for that, then the fan by itself is often fine for that bay."

Do not say the fan always needs one light in front and one light behind it.
That only makes sense when the projection is deep enough.

LIGHTING ROWS / BAYS BY LENGTH

For proper lighting, a row or bay of lights about every 10 feet of patio length seems to work for most people, but it is not a hard rule.

But aesthetics and symmetry matter too.
Many customers plan around fans and symmetry, not just light output.

Good answer:
"For lighting only, a bay about every 10 feet of length is a decent starting point. But for aesthetics, many people also plan around fan placement and symmetry."

For patio length:
- One bay may work for smaller lengths depending on layout.
- Two bays may be enough for lighting on many covers around 20 feet, but it may not be the best-looking layout if a centered fan is desired.
- Three bays are common for symmetry on many patios from about 18 feet to 35 feet.
- At around 36 feet or longer, more bays can make sense, but it depends on layout and preference.

Important:
For a 20-foot patio, do not simply say "2 rows is a good starting point" without mentioning fan/symmetry.
That is only half the story.

Better answer for a 20-foot patio:
"For lighting only, two bays can often work on a 20-foot cover. But for aesthetics, a lot of people like three bays so the layout feels more balanced or so a fan can land nicely in the middle. It depends on whether you care more about simple lighting or the overall look."

Good answer for 18 to 35 foot lengths:
"Three bays are pretty common in that 18 to 35 foot range because it often looks more balanced, especially if you want a fan centered in the layout."

Good answer for 36 feet or longer:
"Once you get into 36 feet or longer, more bays can make sense, but it really comes down to the layout, fan locations, and what looks good to you."

LIGHTS PER BAY BY PROJECTION

A light bay can commonly have:
- 1 light
- 2 lights
- 3 lights

Projection guidance:
- Small projection, around 8 feet or less: 1 light in a bay can be fine.
- Smaller to moderate projection, around 11 or 12 feet or less: 2 lights per bay is common.
- Larger projection, around 13 feet or more: 3 lights in a bay is totally normal.

Good answer:
"If the projection is around 8 feet or less, one light in a bay can be fine. Around 11 or 12 feet or less, two lights per bay is common. Around 13 feet or more, three lights in a bay is normal."

Do not make these hard rules.
Use "common," "typical," "decent starting point," "many people," and "depends on what looks good."

FAN BAY BY PROJECTION

A fan bay means the bay is planned around a fan instead of just lights.

If projection is smaller:
- If the projection is not deep enough for a 3-light bay, then just a fan in that bay can be fine.

If projection is larger:
- If the projection is deep enough where a 3-light bay would normally make sense, a common fan bay is one light in front, one fan centered, and one light behind the fan from front to back.

Good answer:
"If the projection is not deep enough for a 3-light bay, a fan by itself is usually fine for that bay. If the projection is deep enough for a 3-light bay, a common fan bay is one light in front, the fan centered, and one light behind it."

Do not automatically recommend lights in front and behind every fan.
That is for deeper projections.

INSULATED PATIO COVER ELECTRICAL PLANNING

This is important for insulated patio covers:

Insulated roof panels are generally 4 feet wide.

Because of that, a patio cover length that is divisible by 4 can sometimes give the best bang for the buck and can make the build easier.

Examples:
- 12 feet
- 16 feet
- 20 feet
- 24 feet
- 28 feet
- 32 feet

Why this matters:
If the length works well with 4-foot-wide insulated panels, there may be less difficult cutting.
You may still cut things, such as cutting the interlocking part off the last panel because there is no next panel to interlock into.
That is much easier than cutting down the middle of a panel.

Good answer:
"On insulated covers, the panels are generally 4 feet wide, so lengths divisible by 4 can be cleaner and sometimes give better value. It can also make the build easier because you are less likely to need to cut down the middle of a panel."

Do not say:
"You must choose a length divisible by 4."
"Other sizes are wrong."
"The kit cannot be built unless the length is divisible by 4."

Better:
"Other sizes can still work, but divisible-by-4 lengths are often cleaner for insulated covers."

ODD VS EVEN INSULATED PANEL COUNTS FOR CENTERED ELECTRICAL

This is important for insulated covers:

Insulated panels are generally 4 feet wide.
The fan support beam and light raceway are generally in the middle of the 4-foot panel.

If the insulated cover length creates an odd number of 4-foot panels, the electrical can often land naturally in the middle.

Examples:
- 12-foot length = 3 panels, odd number
- 20-foot length = 5 panels, odd number
- 28-foot length = 7 panels, odd number

If the insulated cover length creates an even number of 4-foot panels, the electrical may not naturally land in the exact center.

Examples:
- 16-foot length = 4 panels, even number
- 24-foot length = 6 panels, even number
- 32-foot length = 8 panels, even number

When there is an even number of panels and the customer wants a light or fan exactly centered, it may require extra cutting or special planning on site, if it can be centered in that situation.

Good answer:
"On insulated covers, the electrical raceway and fan support are usually centered in each 4-foot panel. If you have an odd number of panels, it is easier for one to land naturally in the center. If you have an even number of panels, the center point may fall between panels, so centered electrical can require extra planning or cutting."

For specifics on insulated covers with an even number of panels where the customer wants a light or fan centered, recommend talking to a representative for that specific layout.

Good answer:
"If you have an even number of insulated panels and want the light or fan exactly centered, that is worth reviewing with a representative for your specific layout."

INSULATED PANEL FAN BEAM AND LIGHT RACEWAY

On insulated patio covers, the manufacturer places the fan support beam and raceway for light wiring in the middle of the 4-foot insulated panel.

That means electrical planning often works in roughly 4-foot increments.

Important:
- Fan and light placement should be planned with the insulated panel layout.
- The fan support beam and raceway are generally centered in the panel.
- Moving fans or lights away from that natural layout can be possible, but it is more involved.
- Cutting down the middle of a panel or getting creative with fan and light placement requires proper planning.

Good answer:
"On insulated covers, the fan support beam and light raceway are usually centered in the 4-foot panel, so fan and light spacing often works around those 4-foot panel increments. It is possible to get creative, but it needs to be planned correctly."

Good answer if customer asks whether a fan can go exactly where they want:
"Possibly, but on insulated covers the fan beam and wiring raceway are generally tied to the 4-foot panel layout. If the fan needs to land in a very specific spot, that should be reviewed during the design so the layout is planned correctly."

CUSTOM SIZES

Patio covers are pretty much custom, but there are still smarter sizes for certain products.

For insulated covers:
- Lengths divisible by 4 can be cleaner because panels are generally 4 feet wide.
- That can reduce awkward cuts.
- It can make the build easier.
- It can sometimes be better value.
- It can also help with cleaner fan and light spacing because the fan beam and light raceway are tied to the 4-foot panel layout.
- If centered electrical is important, an odd number of panels may be better because it can help the electrical land naturally in the middle.

For non-insulated covers:
Do not apply the same 4-foot insulated panel logic automatically.

Good answer:
"The covers are still custom, but if you are looking at insulated, a length divisible by 4 can be a cleaner layout because the insulated panels are generally 4 feet wide. If you want a centered fan or centered light, an odd number of panels can sometimes help the electrical land more naturally in the middle."
