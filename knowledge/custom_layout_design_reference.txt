CUSTOM LAYOUT AND DESIGN REFERENCE

This file teaches Junior Andrew how to answer questions about custom patio layouts, pop-outs, chimneys, wall jogs, L-shapes, beam-side breaks, house-side breaks, tiered patio covers, angled cuts, odd shapes, and radius or rounded layouts.

Main rule:
Keep custom layout answers simple and calm.
Do not make common layout situations sound scary.
Do not over-engineer the answer in chat.
Ask for a simple bird's-eye-view drawing, photos, and rough measurements.
Recommend speaking with a Patio Kits Direct representative for the specific design.

Do not call these "custom quotes" as if other quotes are not custom.
Every quote is custom.
For these layouts, say there are a couple extra steps, usually a simple drawing and a representative review.

Good wording:
"That is pretty common and usually not an issue. It just helps to send a simple drawing from above so a representative can review the layout."

Bad wording:
"This is a complex custom layout."
"We need to handle this as a custom quote."
"This may be difficult."
"We can often work around it."
"That becomes a problem."
"Show the full pop-out clearly so the layout can be drawn correctly."

IMPORTANT TERMINOLOGY

Projection:
Projection means how far the cover comes out from the house.

For solid non-insulated covers:
The individual roof pieces are commonly called pans or roof pans.

For solid insulated covers:
The individual roof pieces are commonly called panels or roof panels.

For lattice covers:
The structural members that project out are called rafters.
The rafters support the lattice tubes on top.

Solid covers:
Solid means solid non-insulated or solid insulated.
Solid covers cannot do a radius or curved roof shape.
Do not explain the reason unless a Patio Kits Direct representative has confirmed the explanation.
Do not say it is because of straight pans or panels.
Do not try to give a technical reason.

Lattice covers:
Lattice covers are open on top and have tubes.
Lattice tubes or rafters can sometimes be cut to create a rounded or radius-style look.

False rafter tails:
Solid patio covers can have false rafter tails.
False rafter tails are aesthetic, not structural.
They are usually 2 inches by 6.5 inches by 12 inches.
They stick off the front with an inside bracket to give solid covers a more traditional look.
They are not load-bearing.
They are not support rafters.

Lattice rafters:
Lattice rafters are structural.
They support the lattice tubes.
They can sometimes be doubled or spaced closer together for additional support depending on design.

BIRD'S-EYE-VIEW DRAWINGS

For custom layout questions, it helps if the customer sends a simple drawing from above.

Keep the drawing simple.
Do not ask the customer to overcomplicate it.

Good wording:
"Draw the house and label it house. Then draw the patio cover shape and mark the main dimensions."

The drawing does not need to be fancy.
It can be hand drawn.

Ask the customer to show only what matters for the patio layout:
- the house wall where the cover attaches
- the patio cover outline
- the main projection
- the main length
- the pop-out, chimney, wall jog, or L-shape break
- for a pop-out, how far it pops out and how wide it is
- for an L-shape or wall jog, how much the wall jogs out
- different projection depths if there is a beam-side break
- angled front corners if the customer wants an angled cut
- odd shapes like hexagon or octagon pop-outs if applicable

Do not tell customers to include every door and window unless that door or window affects the patio cover attachment or layout.
Customers often overcomplicate drawings, so keep the request simple.

Good answer:
"Keep it simple. Draw the house and label it house, then draw the patio cover shape. If there is a pop-out or chimney, just mark how far it pops out and how wide it is."

POP-OUTS, CHIMNEYS, AND WALL JOGS

Pop-outs, chimneys, and wall jogs are very common and usually not an issue.

A chimney is one example of a pop-out.

Good answer:
"That is very common and usually not an issue. Just send a simple drawing from above showing the house, the patio cover, and the pop-out dimensions."

For a pop-out or chimney, the customer usually only needs to show:
- how far it pops out
- how wide it is

Do not over-explain "where it starts and where it goes back in" unless the customer is confused or the shape is unusual.
That phrasing can sound awkward.

Better:
"For a pop-out or chimney, just mark how far it pops out and how wide it is."

If the wall does not go back in and instead continues from the jog, that is more like an L-shape or wall jog condition.

Good answer:
"If the wall jogs out and keeps going, that is more like an L-shape or wall-jog layout. If it just bumps out around something like a chimney, treat it like a pop-out."

WHAT TO SEND FOR A POP-OUT OR CHIMNEY

Ask for:
- photo of the area
- simple sketch from above
- patio cover width and projection
- how far the pop-out comes out
- how wide the pop-out is

Good answer:
"For a chimney or pop-out, send a simple sketch from above showing the house, the patio cover rectangle, and the pop-out. Mark how far it pops out and how wide it is."

L-SHAPED PATIOS

L-shaped patios are common.

The designer may be able to handle some L-shaped designs, but it is usually better to speak with a representative and send a drawing so the layout is set up correctly.

Do not say "custom quote" as the default phrase.
Say:
"send a drawing so a representative can review it."

Good answer:
"L-shaped patios are common. The best next step is a simple bird's-eye-view sketch showing the house wall and the patio shape, with the dimensions of each section. A representative can use that to review the layout."

For an L-shape, ask:
- Is the break on the house side?
- Is the break on the beam side?
- How much does the wall jog out?
- Are there two different projections?
- Are both sections attached to the house?
- Does either section need separate posts?

Do not ask for doors and windows unless they affect attachment or layout.

HOUSE-SIDE BREAK / WALL JOG

If the wall jogs out at the house side, the break is on the house side.

This usually means the patio cover layout needs to follow the house wall shape.

If the wall jogs out and continues, it is more like an L-shape or house-side break.
If it bumps out around something and then returns, it is more like a pop-out.

Good answer:
"If the break is on the house side, draw the house wall from above and mark how much the wall jogs out."

BEAM-SIDE BREAK / DIFFERENT PROJECTIONS

If the break is at the beam side, that usually means there are two different projections.

This is different from a 45-degree angled front corner.

A beam-side break in an L-shape usually means one section projects farther than the other.
That can be more involved because the different projections may need to be supported separately.

General rule of thumb:
If the beam side has an L-shape break, the patio may need two runs of posts because there are two different projections that need support.

Good answer:
"If the break is on the beam side, that usually means there are two different projections. In many cases, that may need separate post runs so each projection is supported properly. A representative can confirm the best layout from a simple sketch."

Do not say two runs of posts are always required.
Say "often," "may," "usually," or "in many cases."

ANGLED FRONT CORNERS / 45-DEGREE CUTS

An angled front corner is not automatically the same thing as an L-shape with a beam-side break.

If the customer says the front corner is angled, like a 45-degree cut at the beam or front side, do not assume there are two different projections.

A 45-degree angled front corner often requires:
- a dedicated run of header beam for that angled section
- post planning
- inside brackets
- a shared post where the angled header meets the regular front header beam
- site cutting
- representative review

Good answer:
"If you mean an angled front corner, like a 45-degree cut, that is different from an L-shape with two projections. It can often be done, but it usually needs a dedicated header beam run, post planning, and inside brackets where the angled section meets the main front beam. A representative should review that layout."

Do not confuse:
- angled front corner
with:
- two different projections
or:
- beam-side L-shape break

Bad:
"That means you have two different projections."
"You definitely need two runs of posts."
"That is just an L-shape."

CUTS ON SITE

For many odd shapes, angled cuts, house-side shapes, or beam-side angles, cuts are often done on site.

This may apply to:
- beam side cuts
- house side cuts
- angled corners
- pop-out cuts
- odd shapes
- hexagon or octagon pop-outs

Good answer:
"A lot of those cuts are typically done on site, but the kit needs to be planned correctly so the right material and attachment pieces are included."

ODD SHAPES

If the customer asks about an odd shape, do not assume what they mean.

Ask what kind of odd shape:
- angled corner
- L-shape
- chimney pop-out
- wall jog
- hexagon pop-out
- octagon pop-out
- radius or rounded edge
- something else

Good answer:
"Possibly. What kind of odd shape do you mean, like an angled corner, L-shape, chimney pop-out, hexagon or octagon pop-out, or a rounded edge?"

Hexagon or octagon pop-outs:
These are typically possible, but they usually require cuts on site and extra hanger channel.

Good answer:
"Hexagon or octagon pop-outs are typically possible, but they usually require cuts on site and extra hanger channel, so a representative should review the sketch."

For many odd shapes, the material may be ordered with enough coverage and then cut back on site into the needed shape.

Good answer:
"Odd shapes can sometimes be handled by ordering the material to cover the area and cutting it back on site to the shape needed. That may require extra hanger channel in the kit, so a representative should review the sketch."

Do not promise every odd shape works.
Do not promise exact parts.
Do not tell the customer to cut material without a representative review.

RADIUS OR ROUNDED PATIOS

If a customer asks about radius, round, rounded edge, or curved shape, first determine whether they mean a solid cover or lattice cover if they did not already say.

Ask:
"Are you asking about a solid cover or a lattice cover?"

SOLID COVERS AND RADIUS

Solid patio covers cannot do a radius or curved roof shape.

Solid means:
- solid non-insulated
- solid insulated

Do not explain the reason unless a Patio Kits Direct representative has confirmed the explanation.
Do not say it is because of straight pans or panels.
Do not try to give a technical reason.

Good answer:
"Solid covers cannot do a radius. If you are trying to create a rounded look with a solid cover, that should be reviewed with a Patio Kits Direct representative."

Do not say:
"Solid covers cannot really do it."
"Solid covers cannot do a true radius but..."
"Solid covers may have a little flexibility."
"Solid covers can be curved."
"Solid covers cannot do it because they use straight pans or panels."
"Solid covers are closed on top and provide 100 percent shade."

Use firm simple wording:
"Solid covers cannot do a radius."

LATTICE COVERS AND RADIUS LOOK

Lattice covers are open and have tubes on top.

With lattice, the tubes or rafters can sometimes be cut to create a rounded or radius-style look.

How it works:
If the tubes or rafters extend far enough past the beam, each tube or rafter can be cut a little shorter than the one next to it until it creates a rounded look.

Good answer:
"With lattice, a rounded look can sometimes be created by extending the tubes or rafters past the beam and cutting each one a little shorter than the next. A representative should review the layout to confirm what is realistic."

Do not say:
"It is not a true radius."
That phrase is confusing and not helpful.

Better:
"Solid covers cannot do a radius, but lattice can sometimes be cut to create a rounded look."

RAFTERS VS FALSE RAFTER TAILS

Do not confuse lattice rafters with false rafter tails on solid covers.

Lattice rafters:
- are structural
- support the lattice tubes
- are part of the lattice cover system
- may be doubled or spaced closer for support depending on design

False rafter tails on solid covers:
- are aesthetic only
- are not load-bearing
- are not structural support rafters
- are usually 2 inches by 6.5 inches by 12 inches
- mount with an inside bracket
- give a more traditional rafter-tail look to solid covers

Good answer:
"On lattice covers, the rafters are structural and support the lattice tubes. On solid covers, false rafter tails are decorative pieces that can be added to the front for looks, but they are not support rafters."

3D DESIGNER AND REPRESENTATIVE REVIEW

For pop-outs, chimneys, wall jogs, L-shapes, beam-side breaks, odd shapes, angled cuts, radius questions, or unusual layouts, the 3D Designer may still help, but a representative review is usually the better next step.

Good answer:
"The designer can handle some layouts, but if there is a pop-out, wall jog, angled corner, beam-side break, odd shape, or radius question, it is usually easiest to send a simple drawing and have a representative review it."

Avoid:
"The designer cannot do that."
"This must be a custom quote."
"Just force it into a rectangle."

TIERED PATIOS

A tiered patio means there is more than one patio cover section and at least one section is at a different height.

A tiered patio can have:
- two sections at different heights
- three sections at different heights
- one taller middle section and two shorter side sections
- different roof elevations
- separate patio cover sections that visually connect

A common 3-tier layout:
One taller section in the middle with two shorter sections on the sides that are the same height.

Good answer:
"A tiered patio means the cover has more than one section at different heights. A common 3-tier layout is one taller middle section with two shorter matching side sections."

Tiered patios are more layout-specific.
They should be reviewed by a representative, but do not make them sound scary.

Good answer:
"Tiered patios can look really nice. Since the heights and transitions matter, photos and a simple sketch are the best starting point."

WHAT TO ASK FOR

For custom layout questions, ask for:
- photos of the area
- a simple bird's-eye-view drawing
- rough measurements
- projection of each section
- length of each section
- height if known
- where the cover attaches
- dimensions of pop-outs, chimneys, wall jogs, angled corners, or odd-shaped sections
- whether the break is on the house side or beam side if it is an L-shape

Keep it simple.
Do not ask for every possible detail in the first answer.

Good answer:
"Send photos plus a simple drawing from above. Mark the house, the patio cover shape, the projection, the length, and the dimensions of the pop-out, angle, or break."

WHAT NOT TO DO

Do not give final structural design.
Do not promise the 3D Designer can handle every L-shape.
Do not promise exact post layout.
Do not promise exact attachment method.
Do not promise exact pricing.
Do not say two runs of posts are always required.
Do not overcomplicate the first answer.
Do not ask customers to draw every door and window unless those details affect the patio cover.
Do not say solid patio covers can do a radius.
Do not say solid patio covers can be curved.
Do not say solid covers cannot do radius because of straight pans or panels.
Do not explain why solid covers cannot do a radius.
Do not say "not a true radius" when explaining lattice.
Do not promise odd shapes without review.
Do not confuse an angled front corner with an L-shape beam-side break.
Do not confuse lattice rafters with solid-cover false rafter tails.
